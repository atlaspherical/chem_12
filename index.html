Foundations of Redox
▸ Oxidation = loss of electrons; Reduction = gain of electrons. Remember OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. In any redox reaction both processes occur simultaneously.
▸ Oxidation numbers are assigned charges that track electron movement. Use the rules (O is usually −2, H is +1 in compounds, sum = charge on species) to identify what's oxidised (oxidation number increases) and reduced (decreases).
▸ Half equations in acidic conditions: Balance atoms first (add H₂O for O, H⁺ for H), then balance charge with electrons. E.g., MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O.
▸ Balanced redox equations are formed by combining half equations so electrons cancel. Multiply each half equation to equalise electron count, then add.
▸ Displacement reactions: A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal ion from solution (e.g., Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu). Halogens displace less reactive halide ions (Cl₂ displaces Br⁻ and I⁻).
Galvanic Cells & Standard Reduction Potentials
▸ Galvanic (voltaic) cells convert chemical potential energy into electrical energy via spontaneous redox reactions.
▸ Key features: Two half-cells connected by an external circuit (electron flow) and a salt bridge (ion flow to maintain charge balance). The anode is where oxidation occurs (negative in a galvanic cell); the cathode is where reduction occurs (positive). Remember: An Ox, Red Cat.
▸ Salt bridge contains an inert electrolyte (e.g., KNO₃). Anions migrate towards the anode half-cell; cations migrate towards the cathode half-cell — this prevents charge build-up that would stop the cell.
▸ Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) is the universal reference: H⁺(1 mol L⁻¹) | H₂(g, 100 kPa) at 298 K, assigned E° = 0.00 V. All standard reduction potentials are measured relative to it.
▸ Standard conditions: 1 mol L⁻¹ solution, 298 K, 100 kPa. Reduction potentials (E°) in the Standard Reduction Potential (SRP) table are all written as reduction half-reactions.
▸ Cell voltage: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. A positive E°cell means the reaction is spontaneous. A negative value means non-spontaneous (energy input required).
▸ Oxidising and reducing agents: Species with more positive E° are stronger oxidising agents (more easily reduced). Species with more negative E° are stronger reducing agents (more easily oxidised). The SRP table lets you predict feasibility.
Applications of Galvanic Cells
▸ Dry corrosion of iron: Fe is oxidised by O₂ at the surface; water is not required. Forms iron oxides directly.
▸ Wet (electrochemical) corrosion of iron: requires both water and O₂; iron acts as the anode (oxidised), and oxygen is reduced at the cathode region. Accelerated by salt, acid, or contact with less active metals.
▸ Corrosion prevention: barrier methods (paint, grease, galvanising), alloying, cathodic protection (connect Fe to a more active metal such as Mg or Zn — the iron becomes the cathode and doesn't oxidise), and sacrificial anodes (a more reactive metal is deliberately oxidised instead of the iron).
▸ Primary batteries (e.g., Leclanché/dry cell): not rechargeable. Zn anode, MnO₂/NH₄Cl electrolyte, carbon cathode.
▸ Secondary batteries (e.g., lead-acid accumulator): rechargeable. During discharge Pb is oxidised (anode) and PbO₂ is reduced (cathode). Charging reverses this using external electrical energy.
▸ Hydrogen fuel cell: H₂ is oxidised at the anode (H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻), O₂ is reduced at the cathode (O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O). Continuous supply of fuel means continuous electricity — unlike a battery it doesn't go flat.
Electrolytic Cells
▸ Electrolysis uses external electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. E°cell is negative for electrolytic cells.
▸ Key features: The anode is connected to the positive terminal (oxidation occurs), cathode to the negative terminal (reduction occurs). Ion flow in the electrolyte; electron flow in the external circuit.
▸ Molten salts vs. aqueous solutions: Molten NaCl gives Na(l) and Cl₂(g). Aqueous NaCl (brine) gives H₂(g) at cathode (H₂O reduced preferentially over Na⁺) and Cl₂(g) at anode — different products due to competing discharge reactions.
▸ Galvanic vs. electrolytic cells: Galvanic — spontaneous, converts chemical → electrical; anode is negative. Electrolytic — non-spontaneous, converts electrical → chemical; anode is positive.
▸ Industrial applications: Silver electroplating (Ag⁺ reduced at cathode onto object), copper refining (impure copper anode dissolves, pure copper deposits at cathode).
★ SIS — Practical: Conduct electrochemical cell experiments safely, collect valid data, organise and process results to compare reactivity of substances.

Reaction Rates & Collision Theory
▸ Collision theory: Reactions occur when particles collide with sufficient energy (≥ activation energy, Ea) and the correct orientation. Increasing collision frequency or energy increases rate.
▸ Factors affecting rate: Increasing concentration or pressure → more collisions per second → faster rate. Increasing temperature → more particles have energy ≥ Ea (Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shifts) → faster rate. Catalysts lower Ea → faster rate without being consumed. Greater surface area → more exposed particles → more collisions.
Dynamic Equilibrium
▸ Open vs. closed systems: Open systems exchange both matter and energy with surroundings. Closed systems exchange energy only — required for equilibrium to be reached.
▸ Dynamic equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Macroscopic properties (concentration, colour, pressure) remain constant but reactions still occur at the molecular level.
▸ Reversibility and activation energy: Both forward and reverse reactions have an activation energy. The exothermic direction has a lower Ea; the relative position of equilibrium depends on the difference in energy between products and reactants.
Le Châtelier's Principle & Shifting Equilibrium
▸ Le Châtelier's Principle: When a system at equilibrium is stressed, it will shift to partially oppose that stress and re-establish equilibrium.
▸ Concentration change: Adding reactant → forward shift (more product formed). Removing product → forward shift. Adding product → reverse shift.
▸ Temperature change: Increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction (absorbs excess heat). Decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction. Temperature changes alter the value of K.
▸ Pressure/volume change (gases): Increasing pressure (decreasing volume) shifts equilibrium towards the side with fewer moles of gas to reduce pressure. Equal moles of gas on both sides: no shift.
▸ Catalysts: Speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally. Equilibrium is reached faster but the position of equilibrium (and K) is unchanged.
Equilibrium Constants
▸ Equilibrium law expression: For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD: K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Pure solids and liquids are omitted (activity = 1). This is a homogeneous expression when all species are in the same phase; heterogeneous when phases differ.
▸ Interpreting K: K >> 1 → mostly products at equilibrium. K << 1 → mostly reactants. K ≈ 1 → roughly equal amounts. K is constant at constant temperature; only temperature changes K.
▸ Concentration-time graphs: Show reactant concentrations decreasing and product concentrations increasing until equilibrium is reached (lines flatten). Disturbances cause sudden changes followed by re-levelling.
Industrial Application: Haber Process
▸ Haber Process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. Forward reaction is exothermic and produces fewer moles of gas.
▸ Compromise conditions: Low temperature favours equilibrium yield (more NH₃) but slows rate → compromise ~450°C. High pressure favours yield and rate → ~200 atm used. Iron catalyst maintains acceptable rate at moderate temperature. Continuous removal of NH₃ shifts equilibrium forward.
★ SHE — CO₂ and climate: Evaluate how rising CO₂ emissions affect global temperatures (greenhouse effect), ocean pH (ocean acidification via CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃), and marine life (shell dissolution, bleaching).
★ SIS — Practical: Conduct equilibrium experiments safely; collect valid data on effects of changes to equilibrium systems.

+
Brønsted-Lowry pH calculations Buffers Kw & Ka Indicators Titrations
Definitions & Strength
▸ Brønsted-Lowry model: An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor. This applies in aqueous and non-aqueous situations.
▸ Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) fully ionise in water. Weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₃PO₄, organic acids) partially ionise — an equilibrium exists. Same for bases: strong (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂) vs. weak (NH₃, Na₂CO₃).
▸ Strong ≠ concentrated. Strength refers to degree of ionisation; concentration refers to amount of solute per litre. A dilute strong acid can still be 100% ionised; a concentrated weak acid may still be only 1% ionised.
▸ Ka (acidity constant) = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]. A larger Ka means a stronger weak acid. pKa = −log Ka; lower pKa = stronger acid. For equimolar solutions, higher Ka → lower pH.
▸ Conjugate acid-base pairs differ by one H⁺. E.g., CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
▸ Monoprotic vs. polyprotic acids: Monoprotic donate one H⁺ (HCl). Polyprotic donate more than one (H₂SO₄ donates two, H₃PO₄ donates three) — each donation has a separate Ka.
Water, pH and Calculations
▸ Self-ionisation of water: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. Water is a very weak electrolyte.
▸ pH scale: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Acidic: pH < 7; neutral: pH = 7; basic: pH > 7 (at 25°C). Inverse logarithmic — each unit change = 10× change in [H⁺].
▸ Calculations: [H⁺] → pH: pH = −log[H⁺]. pH → [H⁺]: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. Find [OH⁻] from Kw: [OH⁻] = Kw / [H⁺]. Neutral pH changes with temperature because Kw is temperature-dependent — at 37°C, neutral pH ≈ 6.8.
▸ Mixing strong acids and bases: Calculate moles of H⁺ and OH⁻, find excess, calculate new concentration in new total volume, then find pH.
Salt Hydrolysis & Buffers
▸ Salt hydrolysis: Salts of weak acids/strong bases → basic solution (e.g., CH₃COONa). Salts of strong acids/weak bases → acidic solution (e.g., NH₄Cl). Salts of strong acid/strong base → neutral (e.g., NaCl).
▸ Equivalence point ≠ always pH 7 because of hydrolysis of the salt formed.
▸ Buffer solutions resist pH change upon addition of small amounts of acid or base. Made from a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid) in comparable concentrations.
▸ How buffers work: Add H⁺ → reacts with the base component (A⁻ + H⁺ → HA). Add OH⁻ → reacts with the acid component (HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O). pH changes only slightly because the equilibrium rebalances.
▸ Buffering capacity depends on concentration of the buffer components (higher concentration = greater capacity) and the ratio of acid to conjugate base (best when approximately equal).
Indicators & Titration Curves
▸ Acid-base indicators are weak acids (HIn) where the protonated and deprotonated forms have different colours. Colour change occurs over a pH range of approximately pKₐ(In) ± 1.
▸ Equivalence point vs. endpoint: Equivalence point = moles of acid exactly equal moles of base. Endpoint = indicator changes colour. Choose an indicator whose endpoint falls within the steep region of the pH curve near equivalence point.
▸ pH curves: Strong acid/strong base: vertical portion (buffer region) near pH 7. Weak acid/strong base: equivalence point > 7 (use phenolphthalein, range 8.2–10). Strong acid/weak base: equivalence point < 7 (use methyl orange, range 3.1–4.4).
Volumetric Analysis
▸ Principles: Known concentration of one solution (standard) is used to find unknown concentration of another via neutralisation.
▸ Primary standard: High purity, stable (doesn't absorb CO₂ or moisture), high molar mass (minimises weighing error), readily soluble. Example: anhydrous Na₂CO₃ or oxalic acid.
▸ Titration calculation: n(acid) = c × V; use mole ratio from equation; c(base) = n(base) / V(base). Always convert volumes to litres.
▸ Back titration: Used when the analyte doesn't react directly with the titrant, or is insoluble. Add excess reagent, then titrate the unreacted excess. E.g., finding CaCO₃ content by adding excess HCl and back-titrating unused HCl with NaOH.
▸ Experimental errors: Systematic errors (consistent bias — e.g., dirty burette, incorrect indicator) affect accuracy. Random errors (unpredictable — e.g., parallax in reading meniscus) affect precision. Percentage error = (absolute error / true value) × 100%.
▸ Minimising errors: Rinse all glassware with the solution to be used, use a white tile, read meniscus at eye level, take multiple concordant readings (within 0.1 mL), use a primary standard to prepare/standardise solutions.
★ SIS — Practical: Establish properties of acids and bases experimentally. Demonstrate volumetric competence; collect accurate and precise titration data; write scientific reports using appropriate language and format.

Revision: Hydrocarbons & Isomers
▸ IUPAC naming revision for alkanes, cycloalkanes, alkenes, cycloalkenes, alkynes, alkyl groups, haloalkanes (up to C8). Know straight-chain and branched structures.
▸ Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures. Chain isomers differ in the carbon skeleton; position isomers differ in functional group placement; geometric (cis-trans) isomers differ in arrangement around a C=C double bond.
▸ Aromatic compounds contain a benzene ring (C₆H₆) — a planar, cyclic structure with delocalised π electrons. Named as derivatives of benzene.
Functional Groups & Nomenclature
▸ Functional groups are atoms or bonds responsible for characteristic reactivity. Identify: alkenes (C=C), alcohols (–OH), aldehydes (–CHO), ketones (C=O internal), carboxylic acids (–COOH), esters (–COO–), amines (–NH₂), amides (–CONH–).
▸ IUPAC naming rules (up to C8, simple branching): Identify longest chain containing the functional group as parent chain. Number to give functional group lowest locant. Use suffixes: -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -oate, -amine, -amide, -ene.
Reactions of Functional Groups
▸ Alkenes — addition reactions: C=C reacts with Br₂/bromine water (decolorises from orange-brown to colourless → confirms C=C). Also react with H₂ (hydrogenation), HX (hydrohalogenation), H₂O (hydration → alcohol).
▸ Alcohols — combustion: Complete combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O. Incomplete gives CO or C.
▸ Oxidation of alcohols: Acidified MnO₄⁻ (purple → colourless) or Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange → green) are oxidising agents. Primary alcohol → aldehyde (mild/controlled) → carboxylic acid (excess oxidant). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Tertiary alcohol → no reaction with these oxidising agents (no H on C bearing –OH).
▸ Carboxylic acid reactions: React with metals, carbonates, and bases (acid-base reactions). React with alcohols in the presence of acid catalyst (H₂SO₄) to form esters — condensation reaction releasing H₂O (esterification).
Intermolecular Forces & Physical Properties
▸ Dispersion forces: Present in all molecules; strength increases with molecular size/surface area. Only forces in non-polar molecules (alkanes, alkenes).
▸ Dipole-dipole interactions: In polar molecules (e.g., haloalkanes, aldehydes, ketones). Stronger than dispersion for similar molar mass.
▸ Hydrogen bonding: Between H bonded to F, O, or N, and a lone pair on F, O, or N of another molecule. Alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines, amides can hydrogen bond. Much higher boiling point than expected from molar mass alone.
▸ Boiling point trends: Greater intermolecular forces → higher boiling point. Solubility in water: "like dissolves like" — polar/H-bonding molecules dissolve well in water; non-polar molecules prefer non-polar (organic) solvents.
Empirical & Molecular Formulae
▸ Empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. From combustion analysis: convert masses of CO₂ and H₂O to moles, find moles C and H, calculate mole ratio.
▸ Molecular formula = actual number of each atom. Divide molar mass by empirical formula mass to find the multiplier.
▸ Combine formula data with reaction results (e.g., does it decolourise bromine water? does it react with Na?) to suggest a possible structure.
Polymers
▸ Addition polymerisation: Monomers with C=C join without losing atoms. Polyethene from ethene; polytetrafluoroethene (PTFE/Teflon) from tetrafluoroethene. The double bond opens to form the polymer chain.
▸ Condensation polymerisation: Monomers with two functional groups react, releasing a small molecule (usually H₂O). Polyesters: diol + dicarboxylic acid → ester linkages (e.g., PET/polyethylene terephthalate). Polyamides: diamine + dicarboxylic acid → amide linkages (e.g., nylon).
▸ Predict monomer from polymer and vice versa: For addition, identify repeating unit and re-insert a C=C. For condensation, split at ester or amide links, adding H and OH (or H₂O back).
▸ Polymer properties and uses: Crosslinking → harder, less flexible. Longer chains → stronger. Intermolecular forces between chains → determines softening point. E.g., PTFE is non-stick (non-polar, very low friction); nylon is strong and flexible (H-bonding between chains); PET for drink bottles (semi-crystalline, strong).
Amino Acids & Proteins
▸ α-Amino acids structure: Central carbon bonded to –NH₂, –COOH, –H, and an R group (side chain). The R group determines properties of each amino acid.
▸ Zwitterions: At physiological pH, the –COOH donates its proton to –NH₂, giving –COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺ on the same molecule (net zero charge but internally charged). Solution pH can affect which form predominates.
▸ Peptide (amide) bond formation: The –COOH of one amino acid reacts with the –NH₂ of another, releasing H₂O (condensation). The –CO–NH– linkage is a peptide bond. Two amino acids → dipeptide; chain → polypeptide → protein.
▸ Primary structure: The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain (encoded by DNA).
▸ Secondary structure: Local folding patterns — α-helix (spiral coil held by H-bonds between C=O and N–H of backbone, 4 residues apart) and β-sheet (extended chains side-by-side, H-bonds perpendicular). Determined by backbone H-bonding.
▸ Tertiary structure: Overall 3D fold of the whole polypeptide chain, determined by interactions between R groups: disulfide bonds (covalent, between Cys), ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, dipole-dipole interactions, and dispersion forces.
▸ Protein Data Bank (PDB): A global repository of 3D structural data for proteins and nucleic acids, freely accessible by scientists worldwide.

Reaction Design & Stoichiometry
▸ Multi-step synthesis: Complex molecules often require several reactions in sequence. Each step must be chosen to give the right product with acceptable yield and rate. Consider functional group transformations at each step.
▸ Optimising rate vs. yield: Higher temperature → faster rate but may lower equilibrium yield (if exothermic). Higher pressure favours rate and yield (if fewer moles gas in products). Catalysts increase rate without affecting equilibrium position. Excess/removal of reagents can shift equilibrium.
▸ Stoichiometry calculations: Use the balanced equation mole ratio to convert moles of one substance to moles of another. Limiting reagent: the reactant that is completely consumed first; determines theoretical yield. Find by dividing moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient — the smallest value identifies the limiting reagent.
▸ Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%. Values < 100% due to side reactions, incomplete reactions, losses during transfer/purification.
▸ Overall yield of multi-step synthesis: Multiply the fractional yields of each step together. E.g., 80% × 70% × 90% = 50.4% overall. This shows why minimising steps is important.
Industrial Processes
▸ Haber Process (NH₃): Compromise conditions: ~450°C (rate-yield balance), ~200 atm (favours products — fewer moles gas), Fe catalyst, continuous removal of NH₃.
▸ Contact Process (H₂SO₄): Key step SO₂ + ½O₂ ⇌ SO₃. Catalyst: V₂O₅, ~450°C, ~1–2 atm. SO₃ then absorbed into H₂SO₄ to form oleum, then diluted — not directly into water (too exothermic and forms acid mist).
▸ Biodiesel production: Triglycerides (fats/oils) + methanol → fatty acid methyl esters (FAME = biodiesel) + glycerol. Base-catalysed (NaOH) is faster and cheaper but requires dry, low-acid feedstock. Lipase-catalysed is slower, more expensive, but milder conditions, can use higher free fatty acid feedstock, enzyme is recoverable. Evaluate sustainability: renewable resource, lower net CO₂, but land-use concerns.
Enzymes as Biological Catalysts
▸ Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts — they lower activation energy and are highly specific (lock-and-key or induced-fit model). Benefits: operate at mild temperature and pH, biodegradable, highly selective. Drawbacks: sensitive to temperature/pH, expensive to produce, may be inhibited.
▸ Optimal conditions: Most enzymes have a narrow optimal temperature (~37°C for human enzymes) and pH range. Too high temperature → denaturation (tertiary structure disrupted, active site changes shape). Wrong pH → disrupts ionic/H-bonds holding 3D structure.
Ethanol Production
▸ Fermentation: Glucose (from starch/sugars) → ethanol + CO₂, catalysed by yeast enzymes (zymase). Conditions: anaerobic, ~35°C, pH ~4–5. Renewable feedstock; low energy input. Disadvantages: slow, dilute product (~15% ethanol max), batch process, seasonal feedstock.
▸ Hydration of ethene: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH. Catalyst: H₃PO₄ on silica at ~300°C, 70 atm. Feedstock: crude oil-derived ethene (non-renewable). Advantages: faster, continuous, purer product. Disadvantage: non-renewable, higher energy, equilibrium yield ~5% per pass.
Ethyl Ethanoate (Ethyl Acetate)
▸ Synthetic route: Ethanol (from fermentation or ethene hydration) + ethanoic acid (from oxidation of ethanol via acetaldehyde, or from Monsanto process) → ethyl ethanoate + H₂O (acid-catalysed esterification, H₂SO₄). Used as a solvent in nail polish removers and adhesives.
Soaps & Detergents
▸ Soap production (saponification): Triglyceride + NaOH (or KOH) → glycerol + 3 fatty acid carboxylate salts (soaps). Base hydrolysis of ester linkages.
▸ Soap structure: Long non-polar hydrocarbon tail (hydrophobic) + carboxylate (–COO⁻) head (hydrophilic). The tail dissolves in grease; the head stays in water. Soap molecules aggregate into micelles around oil/grease droplets, enabling removal.
▸ Soap in hard water: Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions react with soap carboxylate to form insoluble scum: 2RCOONa + Ca²⁺ → (RCOO)₂Ca↓ + 2Na⁺. Soap is wasted; scum left on surfaces.
▸ Synthetic anionic detergents (from dodecylbenzene): have a sulfonate (–SO₃⁻) head instead of carboxylate. Calcium and magnesium sulfonate salts are soluble → no scum in hard water. Detergents are preferable in hard water areas.
★ SHE — Sustainability evaluation: Compare ethanol and biodiesel production methods for resource use, economics, environmental impact, and sustainability. Consider life-cycle analysis, land use, food security, and carbon neutrality.
